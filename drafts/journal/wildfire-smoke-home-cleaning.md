Wildfire smoke is not just an outdoor problem. When it's thick enough to see in the sky, it's already getting into your home through windows, door gaps, and your ventilation system.

Once it's inside, it settles. The particles are tiny and sticky. They land on walls, fabric, carpet, and inside your HVAC ducts. The smell can linger for weeks if you don't clean the right things in the right order.

Here's where to start.

## Change Your Filters First

Before you clean anything else, swap out your furnace filter and any standalone air purifier filters. A clogged filter keeps recirculating the same smoky air through your home. This is the first step, not an afterthought.

If you have a central HVAC system, consider having the ducts inspected. Smoke residue inside ductwork will keep releasing particles every time the system runs.

## Wipe Hard Surfaces

Smoke leaves a fine, oily film on hard surfaces. You'll notice it most on:

- **Kitchen cabinets and counters**: Wipe with a damp cloth and a small amount of dish soap. The oil in smoke residue responds to dish soap the same way grease does.
- **Walls and baseboards**: A lightly damp cloth with a drop of dish soap works here too. Don't soak the wall. Work in small sections and dry as you go.
- **Windows and sills**: These collect residue quickly because they're often the entry point. Clean the glass, the frame, and especially the sill.
- **Light fixtures and ceiling fans**: Smoke rises and collects at the top of the room. Ceiling fans spread particles if they're running while smoke is present. Wipe the blades before you turn it on again.

## Fabric Holds Smoke Longer Than Anything Else

This is where the smell usually lives. Curtains, upholstered furniture, cushion covers, throw blankets, even your mattress can hold smoke odour for a long time.

- **Curtains and removable covers**: Wash them. Use your regular detergent and add half a cup of baking soda to the cycle.
- **Upholstered furniture**: Sprinkle baking soda on the surface, let it sit for several hours, then vacuum it off. Do this more than once if the smell is strong.
- **Mattresses**: Baking soda on the surface, let it sit, vacuum it off. Air the room well while you do this.
- **Carpet**: Same approach. Baking soda, time, then vacuum slowly. Rushing the vacuum means you pull less out.

## Hard Floors

Smoke particles settle on floors too, especially textured or unfinished wood. Sweep or dry-mop first. Then damp-mop with a mild floor cleaner. Avoid soaking hardwood. One pass with a barely damp mop is enough.

## When the Smell Won't Leave

If you've cleaned surfaces and washed fabrics and the smell is still there after a few days, it usually means one of two things.

Either the smoke got into the walls and ceiling and the paint is holding it, or the HVAC system is still circulating it. Both of those are harder to fix on your own.

Repainting with a primer-sealer can help with walls. For the HVAC, professional duct cleaning is worth considering if you've had several days of heavy smoke exposure.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't run ceiling fans during a smoke event.** They spread particles instead of clearing them.
- **Don't light candles or use plug-in scent diffusers to cover the smell.** Masking it doesn't remove it. You're just adding more particles to the air.
- **Don't leave windows open to "air out" the house while smoke is still present outside.** Wait until the air quality index drops to a safe level. Check it at weather.gc.ca before opening up.

## A Note on Air Quality Warnings in Toronto

Environment Canada issues Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) advisories when wildfire smoke is heavy. During those periods, your home is your best protection. Seal gaps where you can, keep windows closed, and run an air purifier with a HEPA filter if you have one.

Once the advisory lifts, that's the time to open windows, air out the house, and start the cleaning process above.
