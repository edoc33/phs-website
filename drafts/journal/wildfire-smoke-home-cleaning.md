Wildfire smoke isn't just an outdoor problem. When smoke sits over a city for days, it gets inside. It comes through window gaps, bathroom vents, and HVAC returns. By the time the air clears outside, your home has already absorbed it.

The smell is the obvious sign. But smoke also leaves behind fine particulate and acidic residue that can stain surfaces and irritate airways long after the event passes. Here's what to tackle, and how.

## Start with your air system

Your furnace filter catches a lot of what came through your vents. Replace it, even if it hasn't been long since the last change. If you have a standalone air purifier, rinse or replace that filter too.

Wipe down your vent covers. They collect a film of soot and dust that keeps recirculating if you leave it.

## Hard surfaces first

Smoke residue is slightly acidic. On painted walls, counters, and cabinets, it can dull the finish over time if you leave it.

Wipe everything down with warm water and a small amount of dish soap. Work from the top of the room down so you're not re-dirtying what you've already cleaned.

**Walls**: Use a barely damp cloth. Too much water can streak latex paint or lift older finishes.
**Cabinets**: Pay attention to the tops. Grease and smoke residue mix up there and get sticky.
**Windows and sills**: Wipe the sill, the frame, and the glass. That's where residue concentrates.

## Soft surfaces hold smoke longest

Fabric absorbs smoke and releases it slowly. You'll keep smelling it for weeks if you don't address it.

**Curtains and drapes**: Wash them if the care label allows. If not, run them through a dryer on low heat with a damp towel for 20 minutes.
**Upholstered furniture**: Sprinkle baking soda, leave it for 30 minutes, then vacuum it off. Repeat if the smell is strong.
**Pillows and cushions**: Air them outside on a dry day with a breeze. Not on a smoky day, obviously.
**Mattress**: Same baking soda approach. Let it sit longer, an hour if you can.

## Floors

Particulate from smoke settles. Your floors, especially carpet, collect what falls.

Vacuum slowly and thoroughly. This is not the pass to rush. Go over each section twice. If you have a HEPA filter on your vacuum, use it. Empty or replace the canister after, so you're not releasing it back into the air next time.

Hardwood and tile floors can be damp-mopped with a mild cleaner. Avoid anything heavily scented. It'll mix with the smoke smell and make things worse.

## What you probably can't fully clean yourself

Some things are harder to reach or just take more than a home clean can do.

HVAC ductwork is one. If smoke was heavy and your system ran during the event, the ducts themselves may need professional cleaning. It's not a frequent job, but it's worth considering after a serious smoke event.

Heavily soiled soft furnishings are another. If a couch or rug took a real hit and baking soda isn't cutting it, a professional deep clean will do more than repeated home attempts.

## A note on older Toronto homes

Older homes in Toronto and Mississauga often have more gaps and less airtight construction than newer builds. They let more in. If your home is pre-1980s, be thorough. Check closets and interior rooms, not just the spaces facing the street.
