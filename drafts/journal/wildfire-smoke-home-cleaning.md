When wildfire smoke rolls into the GTA, most people close their windows and wait it out. That is the right call for your lungs. But the smoke that already got in does not just leave when the air quality improves. It settles into fabrics, coats hard surfaces with a fine oily residue, and leaves a smell that can linger for weeks.

Here is how to clear it out properly.

## What Wildfire Smoke Actually Leaves Behind

Wildfire smoke is not the same as cigarette smoke or cooking smoke. It carries fine particles, ash, and chemical compounds from whatever burned, including wood, homes, and vegetation. Those particles are small enough to get through window seals and ventilation gaps. Once inside, they coat surfaces with a thin film that is slightly oily and acidic.

You may not see it. You will smell it.

## Start with Ventilation (When It Is Safe)

Once outdoor air quality returns to normal, open your windows and run fans to flush the stale indoor air out. Check the Air Quality Health Index for your area before doing this. Do not ventilate while smoke is still present outdoors.

If you have a central air system with a HEPA filter, run it on the fan setting for a few hours after the windows are closed again. Change the filter afterward. It will have caught a significant amount of particulate.

## Hard Surfaces

Countertops, appliances, window sills, and furniture surfaces all need a wipe-down. The residue is thin but sticky enough to hold onto dust.

**Counters and appliances**: Warm water with a few drops of dish soap is enough for most surfaces. Wipe, rinse, dry.

**Window sills and frames**: These catch a lot because they are often closest to any gaps. Pay extra attention here.

**Walls and baseboards**: Smoke residue settles low and flat. Wipe baseboards with a damp cloth. For painted walls, a lightly damp cloth or a dry sponge (the kind sold for soot) works without pulling off paint.

**Hard floors**: Mop with warm water and a mild cleaner. Smoke particles that settled on floors will track through the house on feet and shoes if you do not get them up.

## Soft Surfaces and Fabrics

This is where smoke hides longest. Fabric absorbs and holds odour much better than hard surfaces.

**Curtains and drapes**: Wash them if the fabric allows. For dry-clean only items, air them outside on a clear day and then take them in for cleaning.

**Upholstered furniture**: Sprinkle baking soda generously across cushions and fabric surfaces. Leave it for several hours, then vacuum thoroughly. Repeat if the smell is still there.

**Carpets and rugs**: Same baking soda approach. For heavier smoke exposure, a carpet cleaner or professional service will do more than a home treatment.

**Bedding and pillows**: Wash everything. High-heat drying helps with odour removal.

**Clothing left out**: If clothes were hanging or left in a room that was exposed, wash them before wearing.

## The Smell That Will Not Leave

If you have done all of this and the smell persists, activated charcoal bowls placed around the affected rooms will continue to absorb odour over several days. So will bowls of white vinegar left overnight, though the vinegar smell is its own temporary problem.

For serious smoke events, where windows were left open during heavy smoke or the exposure lasted more than a day or two, the residue can work into walls and ceiling materials. That level of cleanup genuinely benefits from professional help. Surface wiping will not reach what has settled into porous materials.

## What to Check Before the Next Smoke Event

**Weatherstripping**: Gaps under doors and around windows let smoke in. A few dollars of weatherstripping tape can make a real difference.

**HVAC filter**: Make sure yours is rated to catch fine particles. Standard filters do not do much with smoke particulate.

**Air purifier**: A portable HEPA unit in your bedroom is worth having on hand for smoke season.
