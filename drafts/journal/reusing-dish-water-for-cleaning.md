In a hot summer, people look for ways to stretch water. Reusing dish water is one of the more common ones. It's not a bad idea, but it depends on what you do with it.

Here's what works, what doesn't, and where to be careful.

---

## What's Actually in Your Dish Water

By the time you've washed a full sink of dishes, the water contains dish soap, food particles, grease, and bacteria. The cleaner the dishes going in, the cleaner the water coming out.

Water from the first rinse of lightly soiled items is fine for a few secondary tasks. Water you've used to clean raw meat off plates or scrub pots with burned food is not.

That's the starting point. Not all dish water is the same.

---

## What You Can Reuse It For

**Mopping hard floors**: Soapy water is soapy water. If you're mopping a garage, basement floor, or outdoor steps, dish water works fine. For indoor hardwood or laminate, skip it. Too much water and soap residue can dull the finish over time.

**Watering outdoor plants**: This is the most common reuse, and it generally works. The small amount of dish soap in rinse water won't harm most outdoor plants. Avoid using water with heavy grease, strong cleaners added, or anything used on dishes that held raw meat. Keep it away from vegetable gardens to be safe.

**Cleaning the garbage bin**: Pour it in, scrub with a brush, rinse. Dish water is perfectly suited for this job.

**Wiping down outdoor furniture**: Patio chairs and tables in July collect pollen and grime fast. A bucket of used dish water and a cloth handles that without wasting a fresh bucketful.

**Pre-soaking heavily soiled items**: If you're about to wash muddy garden gloves, sports gear, or work boots, soaking them in reused dish water first loosens the dirt before a proper clean.

---

## What You Should Not Reuse It For

**Indoor floors with wood, laminate, or stone**: The combination of leftover grease and soap residue leaves a film. On hardwood it can dull the finish. On natural stone it can streak badly.

**Anything in the kitchen**: Once dish water has been used, it carries bacteria. Don't use it to wipe down counters, clean inside the sink, or rinse produce.

**Washing windows or mirrors**: The grease and residue in used dish water leaves streaks that are harder to remove than the dirt you started with.

**Doing another round of dishes**: This sounds obvious, but grey water from a first wash is not clean enough for a second round. You'd be washing dishes in bacteria-laden water.

---

## A Few Practical Notes

Don't let reused water sit. Bacteria multiply fast in warm, food-contaminated water. Use it within a couple of hours or pour it out.

A bucket with a lid helps if you're carrying it outside. A splash of greywater on a kitchen floor is its own cleaning problem.

If you're adding anything to your dish water beyond regular dish soap, like bleach, heavy degreasers, or antibacterial additives, don't reuse it. Those concentrations are fine in a sink with running water. They can damage plants, discolour surfaces, and irritate skin when applied directly.
