Some cleaning hacks look great in a 15-second video. They look a lot worse when you're re-grouting your shower or refinishing your floors.

After 30 years cleaning homes in Toronto and Mississauga, I've walked into a lot of rooms where a "hack" made the problem worse. Here are the ones I see most often.

---

## Vinegar on Natural Stone

This is the big one. People pour white vinegar on marble, travertine, or granite countertops because they've heard it cuts grease. It does. It also eats the surface.

Vinegar is acidic. Natural stone is not. The acid etches the finish, leaving dull patches that can't be wiped away. You either live with it or you pay to have the stone resealed or polished.

**What to use instead**: Warm water and a few drops of dish soap. That's it. For stubborn spots, a pH-neutral stone cleaner from the hardware store.

---

## Undiluted Bleach on Grout

Bleach whitens grout beautifully the first time. After a few applications, it breaks down the grout itself. The surface gets powdery and porous, which means it holds more dirt, which means people use more bleach. It's a short cycle to crumbling grout and a bathroom re-grout bill.

**What to use instead**: A paste of baking soda and water, scrubbed in with an old toothbrush. For tougher stains, a diluted bleach solution, maybe one part bleach to ten parts water, used occasionally, not weekly.

---

## Steel Wool on Glass or Stainless Steel

Steel wool removes hard water stains fast. It also leaves fine scratches on glass shower doors and stainless steel appliances. Those scratches catch light and collect soap scum faster than the smooth surface did.

**What to use instead**: For glass, a paste of Bar Keepers Friend and water, applied with a soft cloth. For stainless steel, a microfibre cloth and a little rubbing alcohol, wiped with the grain.

---

## Toothpaste on Chrome Faucets

Toothpaste as a metal polish is a common one. It does have mild abrasive properties, but most toothpastes are too abrasive for chrome. You'll see fine scratches after a few uses, especially on cheaper chrome fixtures.

**What to use instead**: A damp microfibre cloth for daily wipe-downs. For water spots, a little white vinegar diluted in water, rinsed off quickly so it doesn't sit on the metal.

---

## Dish Soap in the Dishwasher

This one usually happens by accident, or when someone runs out of dishwasher detergent and improvises. Dish soap creates far more suds than a dishwasher can handle. The foam overflows through the door seal, sometimes damaging the gasket, and always leaving a slippery mess on the kitchen floor.

One load of improvised dish soap can mean a service call.

**What to use instead**: If you're out of dishwasher tablets, hand-wash the dishes. It's faster than cleaning up the foam.

---

## Lemon on Wood Surfaces

Lemon juice is acidic, the same problem as vinegar on stone. On unsealed or older wood surfaces, it can strip finish, cause discolouration, and dry out the wood. Some people also press lemon halves directly onto faucets to remove hard water deposits, which is fine on chrome, but not on anything with a brushed or matte finish.

**What to use instead**: For wood, a cleaner made for wood floors or furniture. For faucets, the diluted vinegar method above.

---

## A Note on Older Toronto Homes

A lot of homes in Toronto and Mississauga were built in the 1950s through 1980s. The surfaces in those homes, original tile, older hardwood, vintage chrome, are often more fragile than modern finishes. A hack that works fine on a new condo bathroom can do real damage in a 70-year-old house.

When in doubt, test any new cleaner on a small hidden spot first. Give it a few minutes before you judge.
